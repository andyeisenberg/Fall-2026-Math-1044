    Exercises Exercises

    1.

    Suppose a coin has an unknown probability of coming up heads. We perform the experiment in five independent trials, during which it takes 4, 5, 4, 3, and 6 flips to see our first heads in each trial. What is the maximum likelihood estimation for the probability of the coin coming up heads on a flip?
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(5/22 \approx 0.227\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    A particular store owner wants to approximate the average hourly rate at which customers come into the store. They observe 80 customers enter during a particular 4-hour shift. What is the maximum likelihood estimation for the hourly customer rate?
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(20\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    A radioactive material emits particles at an unknown probabilistic rate \(\lambda\) particles per minute. We observe particles emitted at times 1.1, 1.7, 1.3, 2.2, 1.9, and 1.8 minutes. Write the likelihood function \(\mathcal{L}(\lambda)\) based on this data. What is the maximum likelihood estimation for \(\lambda\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\mathcal{L}(\lambda) = \left( \lambda e^{-1.1\lambda} \right) \left( \lambda e^{-1.7\lambda} \right) \left( \lambda e^{-1.3\lambda} \right) \left( \lambda e^{-2.2\lambda} \right) \left( \lambda e^{-1.9\lambda} \right) \left( \lambda e^{-1.8\lambda} \right)\text{.}\) The MLE is \(0.6\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    Suppose a parameter \(\theta\) takes values in \([0, 1]\) with likelihood function \(\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sqrt{\theta} - \theta^2\text{.}\) Find the maximum likelihood estimation of \(\theta\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{2/3} \approx 0. 397\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    5.

    Suppose a coin has probability 0.4 of coming up heads, and we flip the coin 100 times. Let \(S\) be the number of heads. Estimate the probability that \(34 \leq S \leq 44\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\Pr(34 \leq S \leq 44) \approx 0.8212 - 0.0918 = 0.7294.\)
    🔗
    🔗

    6.

    Suppose a fair die is rolled 100 times, and let \(m\) be the average value of the rolls. Estimate the probability that \(3.45 \leq m \leq 3.55\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\Pr(3.45 \leq m \leq 3.55) \approx 0.6255 - 0.3745 = 0.2510.\)

    7.

    The heights of men in the US have a mean of 69 in and a variance of about 9 in\(^2\text{,}\) and the heights of women in the US have a mean of 63.5 in with a variance of 6.25 in\(^2\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Suppose the heights of 30 men are sampled, and a sample mean \(m\) is taken. Find the expected value and variance of \(m\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\E(m) = 69\) and \(\Var(m) = 0.3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Estimate the probability that \(m \geq 69.5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\Pr(m \geq 69.5) \approx 1 - 0.8186 = 0.1814\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    If the sampled group was women, estimate the probability that \(m \geq 64\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\E(m) = 63.5\) and \(\Var(m) \approx 0.208\text{.}\) \(\Pr(m \geq 64) \approx 1 - 0.8643 = 0.1357\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    12.

    In each of the following scenarios, determine whether we should use a 1-tailed test or a 2-tailed test.
    🔗

    (a)

    We have a coin which we’ve flipped many times, seeing an above-average number of heads. We suspect the coin comes up heads more often than a fair coin would.
    🔗
    Answer.
    1-tailed.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    We find a coin on the street and wonder whether or not it’s a fair coin.
    🔗
    Answer.
    2-tailed.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    We suspect there will be a difference in average weight of mice caught during the summer versus during the winter.
    🔗
    Answer.
    2-tailed.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    In a medical study, a group of patients are gathered and the proportion experiencing particular symptoms is measured. A new drug intended to eliminate these symptoms is administered, after which the proportion experiencing symptoms is measured again.
    🔗
    Answer.
    1-tailed.

    13.

    Suppose we find a coin and wonder whether it’s fair. As a first test, we decide to flip the coin 200 times and count the number of heads. If we see 112 heads, should we accept or reject the null hypothesis of a fair coin at a significance level of 0.05?
    🔗
    Answer.
    The \(p\)-value is \(0.1032\text{,}\) so we do not reject the null hypothesis.

The coin from the new factory flips 62 heads in 100 flips. Is this strong enough evidence to reject the null hypothesis that the two factories produce similar coins at a 0.05 significance level?

    19.

    Suppose we find a coin and wonder whether it’s fair. As a first test, we decide to flip the coin 200 times and count the number of heads, \(S\text{.}\) What values of \(S\) would be extreme enough to reject the null hypothesis of a fair coin? If the coin actually has a \(0.6\) probability of coming up heads, what is the power of this test?
    🔗
    Answer.
    (Note: Normal approximation was used for these calculations.)
    🔗
    We can reject the null hypothesis if we see greater than or equal to \(115\) heads, or if we see less than or equal to \(85\) heads.
    🔗
    If the coin has a \(0.6\) probability of coming up heads, then the power of the test is \(0.7852\text{.}\)

    23.

    A store owner wants to determine how much shelf space to allocate to each of the drinks that they sell. They survey their customers about their favorite drinks. Is the data below consistent with the null hypothesis that each type of drink will be equally preferred?
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\chi^2 = 8.5\text{,}\) the critical value with 4 degrees of freedom is \(9.488\text{,}\) so the data is consistent with the null hypothesis.

    24.

    A particular drug is administered in 100 independent trials. In each trial, the drug is administered to four people, and we count how many respond to the drug. The table below shows how many trials have each different count of people who respond to the drug.
    🔗

    (a)

    Is the data consistent with a binomial distribution with parameter \(p = 0.7\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    We have \(\chi^2 \approx 9.888\) without pooling categories, but \(\chi^2 \approx 6.189\) when pooling the categories \(0\) and \(1\) together. After pooling, with \(3\) degrees of freedom, we do not reject the null hypothesis.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    What is the total number of people who have been administered the drug? What is the total number who have responded to it? What is the maximum likelihood estimation for the probability \(p\) that a person will respond to the drug?
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(400\) people have been administered the drug, with \(259\) responding to it. So the maximum likelihood estimation for the probability of responding to the drug is \(0.6475\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Is the data consistent with a binomial distribution with the MLE value of \(p\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    We have \(\chi^2 \approx 2.54\) without pooling categories, but \(\chi^2 \approx 1.24\) when pooling the categories \(0\) and \(1\) together. After pooling, with \(3\) degrees of freedom, we do not reject the null hypothesis.
